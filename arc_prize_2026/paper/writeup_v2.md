# One long trace, closed at a measured cap: verified programs from truncated reasoning on ARC-AGI-2 in 12 hours on four L4 GPUs

*Qwen3.8-27B with no task-specific training; every number is paired over tasks or says which population it comes from; code and artifacts are MIT-0.*

## Summary

We spend the 12 hour, four-L4 Kaggle budget on one long reasoning trace per task from an open 27B reasoning model (Qwen3.8-27B, FP8). A controller sets the token cap of each new trace from the measured decode throughput. A trace that reaches its cap is closed with a fixed sentence and eight programs are sampled from the truncated reasoning; programs are run on the demo pairs, those that reproduce every demo carry the vote. On the 120 public evaluation tasks (166 and 167 test outputs, two independent runs) this reaches top-1 of 41.1 and 39.3 at a 63K cap, against 15.1 for answering with a grid at the same cap (paired +26.0 points, sem 4.05). At the cap the 12 hour budget allows (about 40K) the same data give 32 to 35. The submitted notebook adds two things to that recipe: stream starts staggered 150 s apart under a controller that divides the stream-seconds left by the seconds per token measured on finished tasks, and code-computed facts about the grids in the prompt. Its replica on Kaggle hardware (120 public evaluation tasks at the hidden set's 163.5 s per task, commit mode, four L4) scored top-1 40.3 (95 percent task-bootstrap interval 31.8 to 48.8) and top-2 41.8 at 46.6K decoded tokens per task, against 24.3 for the first replica, and on the box the grid facts raised top-1 at a 32K cap by +12.5 points on the first seed and +0.4 on a replicate seed (pooled +6.5, sem 3.4, mean/sem 1.9), so their effect is positive and not established; the replica pair changed the prompt and the controller at once, and the prompt's pooled effect covers less than half of its 16 points (see the sections on grid facts and on the cap). {The hidden-set score is {lb_score}.}

## Prior work

Greenblatt (2024) reached 50 percent on the ARC-AGI-1 public set by sampling about 8,000 GPT-4o programs per problem and keeping those that reproduce the examples. We keep that check and replace thousands of short samples by one long reasoning trace and eight programs from its prefix. Budget forcing (Muennighoff et al., 2025) closes a model's thinking early to control test-time compute; we use it to turn a prefix into checkable programs and to set the cut from measured throughput. The 2024 and 2025 Kaggle winners trained small models at test time (Akyürek et al., 2024; NVARC, 24 percent on the private ARC-AGI-2 set in 2025); we train nothing and pay with a long decode.

## Why a program, and why one long trace

A truncated reasoning trace usually contains a hypothesis but no answer. Asked for the output grid at that point, the model is right for 4.8 percent of outputs at a 32K cap and was never right where the program arm was wrong (0 of 176 outputs at two caps; `direct_analysis.json`). Asked for a function instead, the same reasoning gives eight candidates that a machine can check against the demo pairs: top-1 at 32K is 31.7 with eight programs and 22.9 with the first one only (`n_completions_eval.json`), and 23 percent of sampled programs pass every demo. The expensive part of the work, finding the rule, is therefore separated from the cheap part, writing and checking its consequence, and truncation costs little because the second part needs only a prefix.

Tokens are best spent extending one trace. Top-1 rises from 13.8 (16K cap, 24.6K decoded tokens per output) to 27.8 (32K, 41.8K), 37.5 (49K, 58.1K) and 40.2 (63K, 70.2K); the slope falls from 0.81 to 0.60 and 0.22 points per 1K decoded tokens. Pooling the candidates of two independent traces of 32K (83.8K tokens) scores 6.5 points below a single 63K trace (70.1K tokens; sem 3.0), and two of 16K score 10.6 below a single 32K trace (sem 2.7). Using one trace per task instead of one per output costs under one point (-0.28, sem 0.65 at 32K).

Universality and progress. The recipe needs a cheap verifier on the task's own examples and a prefix of reasoning. The unchanged pipeline (same prompt with facts, forced programs and voting, at a fixed 32K cap) scores top-1 86.7 (task-bootstrap 95 percent interval 80.0 to 92.5) on 120 randomly drawn ARC-AGI-1 public evaluation tasks, at 32.1K decoded tokens per task: 50 traces stop by themselves (98 percent correct) and 70 reach the cap (78.6 percent correct). Those tasks have been public since 2019, so the figure shows that the recipe carries over to the earlier benchmark, not that unseen tasks are solved at that rate; no other benchmark has been tried. Its measured curve flattens above 58K decoded tokens (0.22 points per 1K), and the 12 hour L4 budget, not the method, sets the score: a stack 1.4 times as fast would reach the measured 63K point (70K tokens per task, 40.2), about 7 points above the operating point, and the flat tail leaves 85 far out of reach of this component alone.

## Grid facts in the prompt

The prompt carries, for every demo pair and the test input, facts computed in code and no test output: grid shape, color counts and a background guess, the number of same-color 8-connected objects with the six largest listed by size and position, whole-grid symmetries, the cells that change between input and output with their bounding box and color changes, and for pairs of different shape the tiling, scaling and sub-grid relations. This adds 1.27K prompt tokens per task (mean; the prompt without it averages 2.79K) and about 3 percent to a task's tokens.

On the 120 public evaluation tasks at a 32,768-token cap (4 x A100, one trace per task) the first run with facts scores 36.4. Two independent runs without them, scored at a fixed 32K cap, score 31.7 and 23.8, and a third made afterwards 23.9. The contrast we pre-registered, facts minus the mean of the first two, is +8.68 points (sd 42.9, sem 3.92, mean/sem 2.22, smallest effect at 80 percent power 11.0) against the +5 we had set for adoption; against the third run it is +12.50 (sem 3.89, mean/sem 3.21). We then ran the pair again with a new seed, both runs at the same time on the same servers (pre-registered before the first task finished): 33.1 with facts and 32.6 without, a contrast of +0.42 (sem 4.83, mean/sem 0.09, smallest effect 13.5). Pooled over the two seeds, facts minus no facts is +6.46 (sem 3.38, mean/sem 1.91, smallest effect 9.5) against the matched runs and +6.74 (sem 3.16, mean/sem 2.13) against all four no-fact runs. Two runs of the same configuration differ by 8.75 points without facts (sem 4.25) and 3.33 with them (sem 3.73), and the four no-fact runs have sd 4.8, so the pooled effect is about as large as the spread between two identical runs. A variant that sends the same facts through the chat API scored 32.4 after reading 45.8 on its first 32 tasks, which is the reason we do not read partial runs. We call the effect positive and not established; the notebook keeps the facts because the pooled estimate is positive and they cost 3 percent more tokens. Split post hoc by the largest grid dimension in a task's examples on the first seed (terciles of 42, 40 and 38 tasks), facts minus the three no-fact runs then available is +5.4 (sem 5.4), +13.3 (sem 6.4) and +11.4 (sem 7.0), and for tasks whose examples keep the grid shape (81 tasks) or change it (39 tasks) +10.6 (sem 4.7) and +8.5 (sem 5.2); these splits predate the replicate and carry the first seed's gain.

The two Kaggle replicas differ by 16 points (+15.97, sem 4.56, mean/sem 3.51), more than the prompt's pooled effect can carry. The mean cap is the same in both (36.9K and 36.8K) and so is the measured decode rate (286 and 299 tokens per second), so the gap is not a throughput gap. At caps of 40K to 60K the staggered replica with facts solved 46.5 percent of 43 tasks, where the first replica solved 31.2 percent of 72 tasks at 40K to 50K. The controller and the prompt changed together; with a prompt effect near +6.5 the remainder is the controller and stagger or run-to-run noise (sd of a difference between two runs about 6.8). The no-facts frontier at the replica's token use is 30.6; adding the pooled facts effect gives 37.0 against the 40.3 measured, 3.3 points above and inside its sem of 4.2.

## The cap is a throughput budget

Decoding runs at 286 to 299 tokens per second on four L4 (vLLM 0.26, TP4, FP8, MTP 2, prefix caching; mean over active time in the two Kaggle replicas), so 240 tasks in 11.5 hours leave about 46K decoded tokens per task: 36K of reasoning and 10K for the eight programs. The KV pool holds about 26 streams at 40K tokens. In both replicas the cache was at least 97 percent full in 34 to 38 percent of the 10 s windows, requests waited in about half of the active windows and decode fell below 150 tokens per second in 26 percent of them, with 24 streams requested and 48 allowed; the stream count is bounded by KV capacity.

The controller therefore works in stream-seconds. The cap of each new trace is the stream-seconds left divided by the seconds per predicted token measured on finished tasks, limited to a rise of 1,536 tokens and a fall of 4,096 from the cap before, and the first 24 streams start 150 s apart so that they do not reach their caps together. The first Kaggle replica started all 24 at once; its caps oscillated and 24 tasks had caps under 20K (0 of 24 solved). The staggered replica's caps rise from 34K to 55K over the first wave and fall to 18K in the last tenth of the run, because the throughput estimate (318, 427, 489, 473, 403, 362, 343, 332, 327 and 323 tokens per second by tenth of the run) was too high in the middle. The stagger changed neither the KV saturation nor the throughput; it smoothed the cap schedule, and by the frontier's shape the spread that remains costs 1.7 points (the frontier averaged over the tasks' tokens 28.9, the frontier at the mean 30.6). The controller cuts the streams still running 25 minutes before the deadline to leave time for their programs. The run used 20,238 s of the 21,780 s it was given. `submission.json` is rewritten atomically after every task and starts from a fallback, so a crash leaves a valid file; the driver restarts the solver (and the servers if one died) and resumes from the log.

## What did not help, with the effect each test could see

- Execution feedback. At 16K the best program was run on the demos and its first failure was fed back. Inside the reasoning (205 outputs, 99 tasks) the model closed its thinking within about 1,000 tokens and scored 2.3 against 23.2 for plain continuation to the same budget (-21.0, sem 3.7). As a fresh chat turn (151 outputs, 90 tasks) it scored 16.1 against 22.2 (-6.1, sem 4.3, smallest detectable effect 12), below the roughly 5 points it needed to repay its extra batch: underpowered below 12 points, not refuted, not adopted.
- Reasoning effort. The chat template offers xhigh (default), medium and low. Medium stops by itself far more often (58 percent of traces within 41K tokens against 4 to 5 percent) but 81 of its 97 stops are abrupt, with no answer, and its forced programs pass every demo in 3 to 10 percent of samples against 22.9. Medium at 47.0K tokens scored 8.5 against 31.7 and 23.8 for xhigh at 41.8K (-23.2, sem 4.0 and -15.3, sem 3.6).
- Serving. INT4 on two TP2 replicas decoded 344 tok/s with the 14 streams its KV cache allowed against 368 for TP4 FP8 at 20 streams, and its NLL on 24 FP8-generated excerpts was higher by 0.0034 nats per token (sem 0.0007). MTP 3 accepted more tokens per draft (1.70 against 1.32) and decoded no faster.
- Richer facts and a picture. A second fact set (object shapes, the seven non-identity plane symmetries, repeat periods, panels, colors dropped and added) scored 36.8 on its first 48 tasks, -3.1 against the first set (sem 4.3). Adding a PNG of the examples through the chat API scored 27.3 on its first 55 tasks, -13.9 (sem 7.0, mean/sem -1.99): 31 of 55 traces stopped by themselves, against 3 of 120 without the picture, and many of those stops carry a program that fails a demo. Both stopped by the pre-registered rule that a variant has to show +4.
- Tool use. An agent that may run Python in a persistent REPL, with its thinking cut at 3,000 tokens to force a tool call, scored 14.7 at a 16K budget (25.0K tokens per task, +0.6 over the program-arm frontier, sem 3.1) and 29.2 at 32K (40.1K tokens, +2.9, sem 4.1); the pre-registered rule asked for +5 at both budgets. On Kaggle it computes 2.34 prefill tokens per decoded token, against 1.12 to 1.18 for the program arm, because decoded tokens are not cached between requests. A variant that adds the grid facts, a check helper and all test inputs scored 17.5 at 16K (+6.0 over the frontier, 114 tasks), and a stopped 32K run scored 36.1 on 60 tasks where the program arm with facts scored 43.6 (-7.5, sem 5.9).
- A hidden-state probe for selecting among samples reached AUROC 0.43 to 0.60 against permutation floors of 0.46 to 0.52 (65 positives): underpowered, not refuted.

## Variance and how to read the board

A single full run carries 3 to 4 points of noise: g5 minus g4 is -2.5, -7.9, -2.4 and -1.8 points at the four caps (sem 3.0 to 4.2, smallest detectable 8 to 12), and four 32K runs without facts scored 31.7, 23.8, 23.9 and 32.6 (sd 4.8). On 2026-10-10 the public board holds 2,682 teams (1,880 above zero) with a median of 28.9 and a dense cluster from 28 to 32 (230, 276, 525, 278 and 123 teams in the integer bins), so 30 ranks 984th, 32 ranks 182nd, 33 ranks 59th, 34 ranks 31st, 35 ranks 23rd and 40 ranks 15th: points that a single run cannot resolve move the rank by hundreds of places. The board's scores come in steps of 1/240, so the public part is 240 outputs and one output is 0.42 points.

## Limits

The public evaluation tasks chose the cap, the voting rule and the prompt, so the replica's 40.3 and the box's 33 to 36 with facts are optimistic for the hidden tasks; the public tasks have also been on GitHub since 2025, and we have no test of whether the model has seen them. The 120-task replica runs the public evaluation tasks at the hidden set's per-task time with a single seed; it checks the controller and says nothing about how the hidden tasks differ. Comparisons across effort levels, feedback variants and prompt variants are single runs against two to four xhigh runs, and the prompt effect itself rests on two seeds. The notebook depends on a public FP8 checkpoint and a vLLM wheelhouse {published as a public dataset}; no internet is used during scoring.
